Christmas Gifts for Family Overseas: Post It, Order Local or Send It Digitally?

By the SendLove team · November 12, 2026

Quick answer

Post a parcel only if it's something that has to come from home and you can send it before the carrier's international cut-off for that country. For heavy or fragile gifts, order from a shop in their country so it skips customs. For photos, letters or anything you're sorting out in the last two weeks before Christmas, send a digital gift that arrives instantly.

Here's how to choose the right route for each person on your list, when to send, and how to avoid the customs surprises that ruin overseas gifts.

Every December the same question lands in group chats between people who live a long way apart: do I post something, order something to their door, or send something they can open on their phone? There's no single right answer. It depends on where they live, how much time you have left, and what the gift is actually for. This guide walks you through the decision, gives you a planner you can follow from mid-November to Christmas Eve, and lists the mistakes that cause most overseas gifts to arrive late, cost the recipient money, or never arrive at all.

What are your options for sending a Christmas gift to family overseas?

Strip away the shopping lists and there are really only four routes. Most people use a mix of two.

  1. Post a parcel from home. You buy or make something, pack it and send it through your national post or a courier. It's the most personal option and the riskiest one in December.
  2. Order from a shop in their country. You buy from a retailer that delivers locally, so the parcel never crosses a border. Faster, no customs, but you lose the "from home" feeling.
  3. Send a digital gift. A photo album, a letter, a playlist, a video, an experience they can book. It arrives the moment you send it and can't be held at customs.
  4. Send money or a gift card. Practical and quick, but on its own it can feel like an afterthought unless you pair it with something personal.

Which option should you pick? A quick decision guide

Work through these questions in order. The first "yes" usually tells you what to do.

1. Is it already past the carrier's international cut-off for their country?

If yes, don't post a parcel. You'll pay for tracking you'll refresh every day and it may still turn up in January. Go to a local order or a digital gift instead. Cut-off dates differ by destination and service, and they're earlier than domestic ones, so look yours up on the official page: usps.com/holiday in the US, the holiday sending page on canadapost.ca in Canada, royalmail.com/christmas in the UK and auspost.com.au/christmas in Australia.

2. Is the gift heavy, fragile or bulky?

Then order it from a shop in their country. Shipping a cast-iron pan or a box of glass ornaments across an ocean rarely makes sense when the same thing can be delivered from a warehouse twenty minutes from their house. Add a message at checkout and send a photo of yourself "wrapping" it so it still feels like it came from you.

3. Is the whole point that it comes from home?

Your mum's shortbread, a local hot sauce they miss, a scarf you knitted, the tea brand they can't find in Sydney. That's what parcels are for. Post early, check the destination's food rules (more on that below), and keep it light.

4. Is the gift really about time, memories or words?

Then a digital gift does the job better than anything in a box. A photo album of the year, a letter they can reread, a countdown to the next time you'll be together. These are the gifts people keep, and none of them need customs forms.

5. Do they genuinely need something practical?

A student in their first winter abroad might honestly prefer money for a coat. That's fine. Just don't send it bare. Pair the transfer with a short message or a small digital gift so it reads as thought, not admin.

Worked example: one family, three countries

Say you live in Toronto. Your parents are in Manchester, your sister is in Melbourne with two kids, and your grandmother is in Toronto too but your brother is spending Christmas in Seoul for work. It's the first week of December.

  • Parents in Manchester: they'd love a bit of home, so you post a small parcel with maple treats and a card this week, well before the UK cut-off for your service. Then on Christmas morning you send a digital photo album of the year, so there's something to open "with you" on the day.
  • Sister and kids in Melbourne: toys are bulky and Australia is strict about what can come in, so you order from an Australian retailer for delivery to her door. For your sister herself, a letter she can read after the kids are asleep.
  • Brother in Seoul: he's in a serviced apartment for a few weeks and doesn't want stuff to carry home. A digital gift plus a message saying you'll take him out for dinner when he's back.

Three people, three routes, nothing stuck at customs. That's the general idea: match the route to the person and the deadline, not the other way round.

What can't you post to another country at Christmas?

Each country sets its own rules, and the carrier's website lists them for each destination. A few categories cause trouble almost everywhere, so treat these as "check first":

  • Food. Homemade baking, meat, dairy, fruit, nuts and seeds can all be restricted. Australia and New Zealand are known for strict biosecurity rules, so food parcels there are the most likely to be held or destroyed.
  • Anything with lithium batteries. Gadgets, toys with built-in batteries and e-readers often have special rules for international mail.
  • Perfume, nail polish, aerosols and alcohol. These count as flammable or dangerous goods for air transport and are commonly refused.
  • Plants, wood and soil. A wreath or a pine-cone decoration can fall foul of agricultural rules.

If you're unsure about an item, leave it out. A parcel with one restricted thing in it can be delayed in full.

Will your family have to pay customs fees on a Christmas gift?

Possibly, and that's the part nobody likes to talk about. Many countries let personal gifts below a certain value in without charges, but the limits are different everywhere and sometimes change. Above the limit, the recipient may be asked to pay import tax, duty or a handling fee before the parcel is released. Nothing spoils a gift quite like your grandmother paying to receive it.

To reduce the chance of a surprise bill:

  • Mark the parcel as a gift on the customs form and describe the contents clearly ("wool scarf, two paperback books"), not "gift" or "misc".
  • Declare the true value. Under-declaring can get the parcel held, and it isn't worth the risk.
  • Split one big parcel into smaller ones only if it genuinely makes sense, and check the destination's rules on that first.
  • Look up the destination's gift allowance on its customs or post office website before you buy, not after.

This is one of the quiet advantages of ordering locally or sending something digital: there's nothing for customs to look at.

What's a good digital Christmas gift for family abroad?

The best ones are personal enough that they couldn't have come from anyone else. A few that work especially well across distance:

  • A photo album of the year. Ask everyone in the family for three or four photos, add a caption to each and send it as one album. Grandparents tend to go through it more than once.
  • A letter. A proper one, not a text. What you've been up to, what you miss, what you're looking forward to. If you're stuck, start with one specific memory from a past Christmas.
  • A countdown to the next visit. If you've booked flights for spring, a countdown turns "see you soon" into something they can check.
  • A recorded video message. Get the kids to sing, show them the tree, walk them round the new flat.
  • Virtual flowers or a card for the relatives you'd normally only send a card to.

How do you make a digital gift feel like a real Christmas present?

Honestly, the biggest risk with a digital gift isn't the format. It's sending it carelessly, at 2pm on a Tuesday, with no context. Some small things change how it lands:

  • Tell them it's coming. "Don't open anything from me until Christmas morning" builds a little anticipation, even for a link.
  • Send it at their breakfast time on Christmas Day, not when it suits you.
  • Open it together on a video call if you can. Watching someone react is half the fun of giving.
  • Pair it with something physical when you can, even a card posted weeks earlier that says "your real present arrives on the 25th".
  • Help older relatives open it. If your dad isn't confident with links, send it to whoever he'll be with, or talk him through it on the phone.

What should you write with an overseas Christmas gift?

A short note does a lot of work, especially if the gift itself was ordered from a shop and arrives with a packing slip. Some lines you can borrow:

  • "I couldn't be there to hand you this, so picture me doing it badly and making you guess what it is first."
  • "This one's from a shop near you, because the post office and I had a disagreement about deadlines. The thought came all the way from here."
  • "Open this with your coffee on Christmas morning. I'll call you at ten your time."
  • "For the kids: Auntie couldn't fly over this year, so she sent her elves instead."
  • "Every photo in here is a year I'm glad I had with you. Merry Christmas, Mum."
  • "It's not the same as being home, but it's the closest I could get. Save me a seat for next year."

What if you've already missed the deadline?

Don't panic, and don't pay for express shipping that still won't make it. You've got two good options left. First, order from a shop in their country; many still deliver locally in the days before Christmas. Second, send something digital, which you can make on Christmas Eve if you have to. If you still want them to get something from home, post it anyway as a New Year or "Twelfth Night" present and tell them it's coming. A late parcel you've warned them about is a nice surprise in January. A late parcel nobody mentioned feels like a forgotten one.

Post parcels early and keep them to things that only make sense from home. Order heavy or fragile gifts from shops in their country. Frequently asked questions

How early should I send Christmas gifts overseas?

Earlier than you think. International cut-offs come well before domestic ones and vary by country and service, so check the carrier's holiday page in November and post economy parcels by late November or early December.

Is it cheaper to send a gift or order from a shop in their country?

For heavy or bulky gifts, ordering from a retailer in their country is usually simpler and avoids customs fees for the recipient. Posting from home makes most sense for small, light things they can't buy where they live.

Will my family pay customs on a Christmas gift?

They might. Many countries allow personal gifts under a certain value in without charges, but the limit differs by country. Mark the parcel as a gift, describe items clearly and declare the real value.

Can I post homemade Christmas food abroad?

Sometimes, but food is one of the most restricted categories, especially to Australia and New Zealand. Check the destination's rules on the carrier's website first, or send the recipe and a photo instead.

What's a good last-minute Christmas gift for family abroad?

A digital gift such as a photo album of the year, a letter or a countdown to your next visit. It arrives instantly and you can time it to Christmas morning in their time zone.
