Adults-Only Wedding Invitation Wording That Doesn't Sound Rude

By the SendLove team · November 18, 2026

Quick answer

The politest way to say no kids on a wedding invitation is one short, warm line such as "We love your little ones, but our celebration will be adults only," placed where guests will see it. Back it up by addressing the invitation to the exact adults invited and showing the number of seats reserved on the RSVP.

Below you'll find ready-to-copy wording for every tone, how to handle exceptions like the flower girl or a newborn, and exactly what to say if someone RSVPs with their kids anyway.

What's the politest way to say "no kids" on a wedding invitation?

Say it once, say it warmly, and say it somewhere guests will actually read it. That's the whole formula. Most of the awkwardness around adults-only weddings doesn't come from the decision itself. It comes from couples hinting instead of saying it, or saying it four times in increasingly stern ways because they're nervous.

Here's the thing: a parent who reads "We love your little ones, but our celebration will be adults only" knows exactly where they stand and has months to book a sitter. A parent who only notices that the envelope says "Mr. and Mrs. Patel" and not "The Patel Family" might genuinely miss it. Hints rely on people knowing an etiquette code that plenty of guests, especially younger ones and guests from other countries, were never taught.

So the approach that works best is a layered one:

  1. Address the invitation to the exact people invited. Names on the envelope, or on the personal link if you're sending it digitally.
  2. Add one short, kind line in the details section, on an insert card, or on your wedding website.
  3. Show the number of seats reserved on the RSVP ("We have reserved 2 seats in your honor").
  4. Tell close family and friends with kids personally before the invitations go out.

Each layer on its own can be missed. Together, they leave almost no room for confusion.

Where should the adults-only note go?

Traditional etiquette says the main invitation card is for the who, what, when and where, and that anything else belongs on a separate card or your website. That's still a sensible default for paper. In practice you've got a few options:

Short "adults only" lines you can copy

These are the ones to use on the invitation card itself, where you've got one line and not much room.

  • Adults-only celebration
  • Adults-only reception to follow
  • We respectfully request an adults-only celebration.
  • Kindly, adults only.
  • Please note that our wedding will be an adults-only event.
  • Join us for an evening of celebration, for grown-ups only.
  • We love your little ones, but this will be an adults-only celebration.

"Adult reception to follow" is common in the US, but read it aloud before you print it. A few people will smirk. "Adults-only reception to follow" says the same thing without the double meaning.

Warm wording that explains why

You don't owe anyone a reason. Still, a gentle "why" softens the message, and for a lot of guests it's the difference between "that's fair" and "that's a bit cold." Pick a reason that's true.

When you want a night out for the grown-ups

  • "While we adore your children, we've decided to make our wedding an adults-only night. We hope you'll enjoy a well-earned evening off with us."
  • "We're keeping our celebration adults only so everyone can relax, dance late and let their hair down. Treat it as a date night on us."
  • "Our reception will run late into the night, so we've chosen to keep it adults only. We hope this gives you a chance to take the night off."

When it's about space, numbers or the venue

  • "Due to limited space at our venue, we're only able to invite the guests named on this invitation."
  • "Our venue has an over-18 policy, so unfortunately we can't include children on the day."
  • "To keep our wedding small, we've had to limit numbers and won't be able to include children. Thank you so much for understanding."

Only use the venue line if the venue really has an age rule. Guests do check, and it's a small thing to get caught out on.

When you're being upfront and friendly

  • "We love your kids, we really do, but this one's for the grown-ups. We can't wait to celebrate with you."
  • "Little ones are so precious to us, but we've decided to make our day adults only. We hope you understand and can join us."
  • "Please take this as your official invitation to a night without bedtimes."

That last one works for relaxed, humorous couples. It falls flat on a black-tie invitation, so match the tone of the rest of your wording.

How do you word it if only some children are invited?

This is where most of the hurt feelings happen, so be clear about the rule you're using. The usual fair lines are: immediate family only, children in the wedding party only, or babies in arms only. Then say it in a way that doesn't invite negotiation.

  • Wedding party only: "Apart from our flower girl and page boy, our celebration will be adults only."
  • Immediate family only: "With the exception of our nieces and nephews, we've chosen to keep our day adults only."
  • Babies in arms: "Our celebration will be adults only, though babies in arms (under 12 months) are very welcome."
  • Ceremony yes, reception no: "Children are warmly welcome at the ceremony. The reception that follows will be adults only."
  • Older children: "We're delighted to welcome guests aged 16 and over."

Pick an age or a category that you can explain in one sentence. "Some cousins, but not those cousins" is impossible to defend when someone asks, and someone will ask.

For the guests whose children are invited, put the kids' names on their invitation or personal link. Don't rely on parents to know they're the exception.

How do you address the envelope or invitation for an adults-only wedding?

The names you write are your clearest signal, so be precise.

For adult children who still live at home, the old rule was that anyone over 18 gets their own invitation. You don't need to go that far, but list them by name on their parents' invitation if they're invited, so there's no doubt.

If you're sending invitations digitally, the same idea applies to the personal link: the guest's name and the number of seats you've reserved should be the first thing they see. For a fuller guide to keeping head counts under control once replies come in, see how to collect wedding RSVPs online.

What should the RSVP say?

The RSVP is your quiet safety net. A guest who skipped your note will still see it when they reply.

  • "We have reserved ___ seats in your honor."
  • "___ of ___ guests attending."
  • "We've saved 2 seats for you."
  • "Please let us know if both of you can join us."

Fill in the number yourself before sending. A blank "number attending" line is exactly where "2 adults, 3 kids" ends up being written in.

Adults-only wording for different invitation styles

Here's how the same message reads across a few common styles. Copy the one that matches yours.

Formal (US and Canada)

Together with their families, Emily Carter and James Nguyen request the pleasure of your company at their marriage. Saturday, the fifth of June, two thousand twenty-seven, at four o'clock in the afternoon. Reception to follow. Adults only, please.

Formal (UK and Australia)

Mr and Mrs David Hughes request the pleasure of your company at the marriage of their daughter Lucy to Thomas Reid. On the insert card: "We hope you'll understand that we have chosen to make our day an adults-only celebration."

Casual

Hannah and Leo are getting married! Come eat, drink and dance with us. It's an adults-only party, so book that babysitter.

Destination or weekend wedding

Our wedding weekend in Tuscany will be adults only. We know that makes travel trickier for parents, so we're sharing the news early to give you plenty of time to plan.

For destination weddings, the earlier the better. Parents may need to arrange several nights of childcare, not just one evening.

Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

  • Saying it more than once. One line on the invitation plus seats on the RSVP is plenty. Repeating "NO CHILDREN" on every card starts to sound like a warning sign at a pool.
  • Negative wording. "No kids allowed" is clear but blunt. "Adults-only celebration" says the same thing more kindly.
  • Hiding it on the website only. Plenty of guests never open the wedding website. Use it for the details, not as the only place you mention it.
  • A vague rule for exceptions. "Close family only" means different things to different people. Name the category or the age.
  • Last-minute news. If you send invitations six to eight weeks out, a parent may struggle to find childcare, especially for a wedding over a holiday weekend or out of town. Mention it on the save-the-date if you send one.
  • Forgetting new parents. If a close friend has a newborn, decide in advance whether you'll make a babes-in-arms exception, and tell them personally either way.

What if a guest RSVPs with their children anyway?

It happens, usually because someone missed the note rather than ignored it. Don't fix it on the RSVP card or by text with a curt "Sorry, no kids." Call them, or ask a parent or close friend who knows them to call.

Something like this works: "We were so happy to get your RSVP. Just a heads-up in case it got missed, we've decided to make the wedding adults only, so it'll just be the two of you. We'd completely understand if that makes it tricky, and we really hope you can still come."

It's friendly, it's final, and it gives them an easy way to decline if they truly can't find a sitter. Some guests will decline. That's an expected part of an adults-only wedding, not a sign that you worded it wrong.

Is it rude to have an adults-only wedding?

No. It's a common choice in the US, Canada, the UK and Australia, and most guests understand it, particularly when you're warm and consistent. Where it gets tricky is family culture. In some families, and in some communities where weddings traditionally include everyone, a child-free wedding is less common and may need a personal conversation rather than a line on a card.

If you're inviting guests from different backgrounds or countries, think about who might be surprised, and call them first. A five-minute phone call before the invitations land does more than any perfect phrasing.

Frequently asked questions

How do you politely say no kids at a wedding?

Use one warm line such as "We love your children, but we've chosen to make our wedding an adults-only celebration," address the invitation to the invited adults by name, and list the number of seats reserved on the RSVP.

Should 'adults only' go on the invitation or the website?

Put it somewhere guests will definitely see, ideally the invitation or a details card. The wedding website is a good place for the longer explanation, but many guests never open it.

Do I have to give a reason for a child-free wedding?

No. A reason can soften the message, such as a late finish or a small venue, but only use one that's true. "We've chosen to keep our day adults only" is enough on its own.

Can I invite some children but not others?

Yes, as long as the rule is clear and easy to explain, such as children in the wedding party, immediate family, or babies under 12 months. Name the invited children on their parents' invitation.

What do I say if a guest RSVPs with their kids?

Call them rather than texting. Kindly mention that the wedding is adults only, say you hope they can still come, and leave them room to decline if they can't arrange childcare.

When should guests find out the wedding is adults only?

As early as possible. Mention it on the save-the-date if you send one, so parents have time to arrange childcare, especially for destination or holiday weekend weddings.
